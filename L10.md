Should remind you of stratified sampling -- different weight in each
(post)stratum -- but now the $n_h$ are not fixed in advance -- so we
have added variability

All weights within a post-stratum are increased/decreased
proportionately so that the sum of post-stratified weights equals the
known population total

For unit $j$ in post-stratum $h$: $$\begin{aligned}
\text{\blue{Poststratified weight}} &= \text{\red{original weight}} \times \text{\purple{adjustment factor}} & \\
\blue{w_{hj}^{PS}} &= \red{w_{hj}} \times \purple{\frac{\text{Population total in post-stratum}~h}{\text{Estimated total in post-stratum}~h}} \\
& = \red{w_{hj}} \times \purple{\frac{N_h}{\widehat{N}_h}}
\end{aligned}$$

Post-stratification aligns the representation of the poststratification
variable(s) to match that of the population

You can poststratify on multiple variables but you must know the

So why would we ever stratify at the design stage?

Technically, the variance of $\ybar_{PS}$ is larger than $\ybar_{str}$
because the $n_h$ are random

Also, guard against a fluke "bad sample\"

If the strata are fixed at the design stage ($n_h$ are fixed), we have
the usual formula:

$\displaystyle V(\ybarstr) = \sum_{h=1}^H \left(\frac{N_h}{N}\right)^2  \fpchpar \frac{S_h^2}{n_h}$

With poststratification, $n_h$ are random variables and we can do some
trickery to get: $$\begin{aligned}
V(\ybar_{PS}) &= E[ V(\ybarstr | n_1, n_2, \dots, n_h)] + V[ E(\ybarstr | n_1, n_2, \dots, n_h)] & \\
&= E\left[ \sum_{h=1}^H \left(\frac{N_h}{N}\right)^2  \fpchpar \frac{S_h^2}{n_h}\right] + \underbrace{V(\ybar_U)}_{\text{\small (= 0)}} \\
&= E\left[\sum_{h=1}^H \left(\frac{N_h}{N}\right)^2  \frac{S_h^2}{n_h} - \sum_{h=1}^H \left(\frac{N_h}{N}\right)^2  \frac{S_h^2}{N_h}\right]\\
&= \sum_{h=1}^H \left(\frac{N_h}{N}\right)^2 S_h^2 E\left(\frac{1}{n_h}\right) - \sum_{h=1}^H \left(\frac{N_h}{N}\right)^2  \frac{S_h^2}{N_h}  \quad (\text{\small only random variable is }n_h)\\
&= \dots \text{using an approximation for } E(1/n_h) \text{ and some algebra}\dots \\
&\approx \underbrace{\sum_{h=1}^H \frac{N_h}{N}\fpcpar\frac{S_h^2}{n}}_{\text{variance under proportional allocation!}} + \underbrace{\sum_{h=1}^H \left(1-\frac{N_h}{N} \right) \frac{S_h^2}{n^2}}_{\text{extra variability due to $n_h$ being random}}
\end{aligned}$$

$\displaystyle V(\ybar_{PS}) = \red{\sum_{h=1}^H \frac{N_h}{N}\fpcpar\frac{S_h^2}{n}} + \blue{\sum_{h=1}^H \left(1-\frac{N_h}{N} \right) \frac{S_h^2}{n^2}}$

Notice that the is of order $n^{-2}$ ($n^2$ in denominator)

Usually the 2nd term is really small compared to the first term

Thus, variance of the poststratified mean (of an SRS) is usually
effectively **the same as variance of the stratified mean under
proportional allocation**!

This holds as long as the total sample size $n$ is "large\" (and it is
in most surveys)

Often we can get the population totals for a set of variables -- age,
race, etc. -- but only know the

In other words, we know the number in each age group, and the number in
each race group, but not in groups formed by crossing age and race
(e.g., 18-25 year old white individuals, 18-25 year old Black
individuals, etc.)

In this case, a method called can be used to adjust the weights to
achieve the correct (true) marginal totals

(You may see raking also referred to as "iterative proportional
fitting\")

In you iteratively adjust the sample weights to match each set of
marginal totals, going back and forth between margins (variables) until
convergence (until all margins "match\" the truth)

This is easiest seen in an example...

Suppose I only know the marginal totals for level and residency:

-   Used extensively in U.S. household surveys where Census or other

Theoretically, could use a lot of variables for poststratification and
raking -- and the more you use, the more bias reduction you can gain

But -- resulting poststrata can end up with small estimated total counts
$(\widehat{N}_h)$, which can make the weights highly variable and
unstable

Options:

-   Collapse categories in the variable(s) used for
    poststratification/raking

    -   Problem: ad-hoc solution, how to decide what gets collapsed?

-   Use a model-based method that can handle more poststrata/raking
    variables and ensure groupings are not too small or weight
    adjustments are not too extreme

    -   Example: OMAS uses something called a Generalized Exponential
        Model (GEM) to simultaneously adjust the weights to a large set
