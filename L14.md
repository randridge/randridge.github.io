  ------------------------------ --------------------------------------------------------------
  True (theoretical) variance:   $\displaystyle V(\that)=N^2 \fpcpar \frac{S^2}{n}$

Generalizing this idea produces PPS sampling

= sample selection methods in which the probability of selection for a
sampling unit is directly proportional to a size measure, $X_i$, which
is known for all units before sampling

We will use throughout this lecture $X_i = M_i =$ number of SSUs in the
PSU

-   I.e., $X_i$ = the "size\" of the PSU = $M_i$

But, other $X_i$ are possible

-   Example: Sampling businesses using $X_i$ = last year's revenue as
    reported to IRS

Note that we want to choose an $X_i$ that is:

-   Proportional to the $y_i$ that we are trying to estimate

-   

= Simplest PPS scheme

-   Conduct $n$ independent draws of a single PSU from the population

-   The probability of selecting PSU $i$ on each draw is proportional to
    $X_i$

-   A PSU **can** be sampled more than once ()

Probability PSU $i$ selected on any one draw =
$\displaystyle \psi_i  = \frac{X_i}{\sum_{i=1}^N X_i}$

New notation: $\psi_i$ ("psi\")

-   $\psi_i$ = probability PSU $i$ is selected on

-   $\pi_i$ = probability PSU $i$ is in the

-   $\psi_i \ne \pi_i$

To obtain a PPS With Replacement, many possible algorithms:

-   Cumulative Size Method

-   Cumulative $\psi_i$ Method

-   Lahiri's Rejective Method

-   others...

Easiest, conceptually, is the :

1.  Create a list of the PSUs with their sizes, $M_i$

2.  Calculate the for each PSU

    -   PSU 1: cumulative sum = $M_1$

    -   PSU 2: cumulative sum = $M_1+M_2$

    -   PSU 3: cumulative sum = $M_1+M_2+M_3$

    -   etc.

3.  Randomly select $n$ integers between 1 and $M_0 = \sum_{i=1}^N M_i$

4.  Sample the PSUs whose cumulative $M_i$ contain these $n$ integers

    -   Select PSU 1 if integer between $(1, M_1)$

    -   Select PSU 2 if integer between $(M_1+1, M_2)$

    -   Select PSU 3 if integer between $(M_1+M_2+1, M_3)$

    -   etc.

2.  Average these $n$ estimated totals to produce the

For the population of schools, my PPSWR sample of $n=2$ schools is:

   School   $M_i$  $\psi_i = M_i/(\sum_{i=1}^N M_i)$    $t_i$      $\frac{t_i}{\psi_i}$
  -------- ------- ----------------------------------- ------- ----------------------------
     8       500   500/14989 = 0.0333578                 251     251/0.0333578 = 7524.477
     23      690   690/14989 = 0.04603376                343    343/0.04603376 = 7451.0533

$$\begin{aligned}
\text{Estimated total} = \text{ average of the } t_i\psi_i = \that_{wr} &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i \in \S} \frac{t_i}{\psi_i} \\
&= (7524.5 + 7451.1)/2 = \blue{7487.8}
\end{aligned}$$ *A PSU can be selected into the sample more than once --
"count\" it each time*

Can rewrite the formula for the estimated total to see the sampling
weights:

$$\that_{wr} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i \in \S} \frac{t_i}{\psi_i} = \sum_{i \in \S} \frac{t_i}{n \psi_i} = \sum_{i \in \S} \red{\frac{1}{n \psi_i}} t_i = \sum_{i \in \S} \red{w_i} t_i$$

Weight for PSU $i$:
$\displaystyle w_i= \frac{1}{n \psi_i} = \frac{1}{\text{expected \# times PSU $i$ appears in sample}}$

For my sample,

   School   $M_i$   $t_i$  $\psi_i$     $w_i = \frac{1}{n\psi_i}$
  -------- ------- ------- ------------ ---------------------------------------------
     8       500     251   0.0333578    1/(2\*0.0333578) = 1/0.0667156 = 14.988998
     23      690     343   0.04603376   1/(2\*0.04603376) =1/0.09206752 = 10.861594

So I can also calculate the estimated total as:
$$\that_{wr} = \sum_{i \in \S} w_i t_i = (14.988998\times 251) + (10.861594 \times 343) = \blue{7487.8}$$
(Same estimate, of course!)

The inclusion probabilities are **not** the inverse of the sampling
weight for this design -- in fact, $P($PSU $i$ in
sample$) = 2 \psi_i - \psi_i^2$ (via addition rule for probabilities)

To estimate the variance of $\that_{wr}$:
$$\widehat{V}(\that_{wr}) = \frac{1}{n} \frac{1}{n-1} \sum_{i \in \S} \left( \frac{t_i}{\psi_i} - \that_{wr} \right)^2 = \frac{s^2}{n}$$
where $s^2$ = variance of the $n$ individual

For the population of schools, my PPSWR sample of $n=2$ schools is:

    School   $\psi_i$      $t_i$   $\frac{t_i}{\psi_i}$
  ---------- ------------ ------- ----------------------
      8      0.0333578      251          7524.477

We know that for SRS, variance is reduced when sampling replacement
(compared to with replacement)

The same holds for PPS!

But, now it is trickier to take a sample because the probability of
selecting PSU $i$ on the second draw **is not the same** as the
probability of selecting PSU $i$ on the first draw

Simple example illustrates this:

-   $P($select PSU 1 on 1st draw$) =\psi_1$

-   but

-   $P($select PSU 1 on 2nd draw $|$ PSU 1 on 1st draw$)=0$

-   $P($select PSU 1 on 2nd draw $|$ PSU 1 on 1st draw$)>0$

Many (complex!) algorithms for selecting PPS samples

You specify the

Which imply the weights:
$\displaystyle w_i = \frac{1}{\pi_i} = \frac{1}{n \psi_i}$ (same weights
as PPSWR!)

Note the implied restriction: b/c inclusion probabilities $(\pi_i)$
cannot be $>$ 1\
Rewritten: which means no one PSU can be so big its $M_i$ takes up more
than $1/n$ of the total of the $M_i$

To estimate the total, use the **Horvitz-Thompson Estimator**
$$\that_{HT} = \sum_{i \in \S} \frac{t_i}{\pi_i} = \sum_{i \in \S} w_i t_i$$

For the population of schools, I used Stata to take a PPSWOR sample of
$n=2$:

   School   $M_i$   $t_i$  $\psi_i$               $\pi_i = n \psi_i$       $w_i = \frac{1}{\pi_i}$
  -------- ------- ------- ---------------------- ------------------------ -------------------------
     14      531     267   531/14989 = 0.035426   2\*0.035426 = 0.070852   14.11
     24      335     166   335/14989 = 0.022350   2\*0.022350 = 0.044699   22.37

My estimate of the total number of females:
$$\that_{HT} = \sum_{i \in \S} w_i t_i = 267 \times 14.11 + 166 \times 22.37 = \purple{7482}$$

The variance of the **PPSWOR** estimator depends on:

-   Selection (Inclusion) probabilities: $\pi_i$

-   $\green{\pi_{ij}}=P($PSU $i$ and PSU $j$ in sample$)$

The $\pi_{ij}$ are **not** just products of the $\pi_i$!

For $n=2$:

-   $P(i$ first, $j$ second$) = P(i$ first$)\times P(j$ second $| i$
    first$) = \psi_i \times \frac{\psi_j}{1-\psi_i}$\

-   $P(j$ first, $i$ second$) = P(j$ first$)\times P(i$ second $| j$
    first$) = \psi_j \times \frac{\psi_i}{1-\psi_j}$\

-   $\pi_{ij} = P(i$ and $j$ in sample$) = P(i$ first, $j$
    second$) + P(j$ first, $i$ second$)$

     
    $= \psi_i \times \frac{\psi_j}{1-\psi_i}+ \psi_j \times \frac{\psi_i}{1-\psi_j}$

$V(\that_{HT})$ depends on the $\pi_{ij}$ -- have to have them to get
unbiased estimate of variance

Complex!

Theoretical variance of $\that_{HT}$ is: $$\begin{aligned}
V(\that_{HT}) & = \sum_{i=1}^N \frac{1- \pi_i}{\pi_i} t_i^2 + \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{k=1, k\ne i}^N \frac{\pi_{ik}-\pi_i \pi_k}{\pi_i \pi_k}t_i t_k \\
&= \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{k=1, k\ne i}^N (\pi_i \pi_k - \pi_{ik}) \left(\frac{t_i}{\pi_i}-\frac{t_k}{\pi_k}\right)^2
\end{aligned}$$

Two possible estimators, plugging in sample quantities to either the top
or bottom

**Horvitz-Thompson** variance estimator (based on 1st equation)
$$\widehat{V}_{HT}(\that_{HT}) = \sum_{i \in \S} (1-\pi_i)\frac{t_i^2}{\pi_i^2} + \sum_{i \in \S} \sum_{k \in \S, k \ne i} \frac{\pi_{ik}-\pi_i \pi_k}{\pi_{ik}} \frac{t_i}{\pi_i} \frac{t_k}{\pi_k}$$

**Sen-Yates-Grundy** variance estimator (based on 2nd equation)
$$\widehat{V}_{SYG}(\that_{HT}) = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{i \in \S} \sum_{k \in \S, k \ne i} \frac{\pi_{ik}-\pi_i \pi_k}{\pi_{ik}} \left( \frac{t_i}{\pi_i} - \frac{t_k}{\pi_k} \right)^2$$

Though it's the same theoretical variance, $\widehat{V}_{HT}$ and
$\widehat{V}_{SYG}$ will not be the same

Several problems with these variance estimators,
$\widehat{V}_{HT}(\that_{HT})$ and $\widehat{V}_{SYG}(\that_{HT})$

-   Publicly available datasets

    -   Not practical! For a sample of size $n$ there are $n(n-1)$
        pairwise inclusion probabilities!

-   For some designs the $\pi_{ij}$ may be hard to calculate

-   $\widehat{V}_{HT}(\that_{HT})$ and $\widehat{V}_{SYG}(\that_{HT})$
    can actually produce **negative** variance estimates for some
    unequal probability designs!

For these reasons, we often use the **with replacement variance
estimate**\
(the one on slide 15)
$$\widehat{V}_{WR}(\that_{HT}) = \frac{1}{n} \frac{1}{n-1} \sum_{i \in \S} \left( \frac{t_i}{\psi_i} - \that_{HT} \right)^2 = \frac{n}{n-1}\sum_{i \in \S} \left( \frac{t_i}{\pi_i} - \frac{\that_{HT}}{n} \right)^2$$

result is (usually slightly) conservative -- i.e., variance too big

don't need pairwise inclusion probabilities, and never get a negative
variance!

$\widehat{V}_{WR}(\that_{HT})$ is what is calculated in most software
(including Stata and R)
